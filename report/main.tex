\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Credit given to smallholder farmers as tokens that can only be spent with approved input
vendors combines a ledger mechanism with an economic bet. This note examines both halves.
On the XRP Ledger testnet, \EnfN{} transaction scenarios show which spending restrictions
the protocol enforces and which depend on key custody, field institutions or the issuer's
discretion. A budget-constrained allocation model, solved exactly and by two greedy rules,
shows that a cheap heuristic is near-optimal only if it prices default risk. An agent-based
simulation of a revolving fund, with parameters from Ghanaian data where they exist and
explicit assumptions where they do not, then asks how much a lender gains from the
repayment history the ledger accumulates. Under these assumptions the history adds
\LedgerLo--\LedgerHi\% to harvest gain in five of six scenarios, custody arrangements matter more than any
allocation rule, and seeing diversion on the ledger adds almost nothing to risk
prediction. All farmer data are synthetic; the results are statements about a model,
written to be tested in the field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and questions}

Smallholder farmers in sub-Saharan Africa rarely borrow: in Ghana's 2005/06 living
standards survey, fewer than 20\% of farming households used any credit and at most 6\%
used agricultural credit \citep{chamberlin2008}. Restricted-spend digital credit is one
response that has been described publicly: a farmer receives tokens redeemable only with
registered providers of fertiliser, labour or storage, and every redemption leaves a record
that can later support lending decisions \citep{cted,nyuad2026}. This note does not
describe or evaluate any deployed system; it studies the design in the abstract, with the
XRP Ledger as the concrete platform. Three questions organise it:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{Enforcement.} Which parts of ``credit that can only be spent at approved
vendors'' does the ledger itself enforce?
\item \textbf{Allocation.} Given a fund, a tolerance for expected loss and per-farmer
default risk, how should credit be allocated, and how close do simple rules come?
\item \textbf{Information.} How much is the repayment history recorded on the ledger
worth to the lender, and does seeing \emph{how} credit was spent add to it?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Code, data and every number in this note are reproducible from the accompanying
repository; tables are generated from the result files rather than typed.

\section{What the XRP Ledger enforces}

\paragraph{Setup.} A fresh issuer, an approved vendor, two farmers and an unauthorised
``stranger'' are funded on testnet for every run. The issuer enables \texttt{RequireAuth}
(only trust lines it authorises may hold the credit token), \texttt{DefaultRipple} (holders
can pay each other through the issuer) and trust-line clawback. Each farmer enables
\texttt{DepositAuth} and preauthorises only the issuer, so that it should be unable to
receive the token from anyone else. All features used were confirmed enabled on testnet
and mainnet from the on-ledger amendments object, not from documentation
\citep{xrpldepositauth,xrpldomains}.

\paragraph{Findings.} Table~\ref{tab:enf} records five results.
\textit{(i)} \texttt{DepositAuth} alone is not enough: a farmer can push credit into another
farmer's account by rippling a self-payment through it, and the receiving farmer signs
nothing (S4a). This was not the expected outcome: the farmer's trust line to the issuer was
created without \texttt{NoRipple}, which must therefore be set explicitly (S4b).
\textit{(ii)} Every farmer-side protection is a flag the account holder can clear (S4c, S5):
whether the restriction holds is decided by \emph{who holds the farmer's key}.
\textit{(iii)} Offers on the open decentralised exchange deliver tokens regardless of
\texttt{DepositAuth}, so credit can be sold for cash (S6a); a permissioned domain restricts
its own order book, not where the token trades (S6b, S6c).
\textit{(iv)} The issuer's freeze and clawback work, but they are blunt and concentrate
power in the issuer (S8).
\textit{(v)} A vendor who accepts credit and hands over cash is indistinguishable on-ledger
from a legitimate sale (S7). The share of credit that ``leaks'' away from inputs is
therefore not a property of the ledger but of the custody model and field auditing; the
simulation below treats it so.

\section{Allocating credit under a budget and default risk}

Farmer $i$ receives one credit level $x_i$ from a menu including zero. With expected gain
$g_i(x)$, concave in $x$, default probability $p_i$, budget $B$, cap $L$ on expected loss
and vendor capacities $C_v$, the lender solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{x}\ \sum_i g_i(x_i)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i x_i \le B,\quad \sum_i p_i x_i \le L,
\quad \sum_{i\in v} x_i \le C_v\ \ \forall v .
\label{eq:alloc}
\end{equation}
The exact optimum is found as a mixed-integer programme (HiGHS via SciPy,
\citealp{scipy}; verified against brute-force enumeration on small instances) in about
\MilpSecs{} s for 1{,}000 farmers. Two greedy rules raise one farmer by one level at a
time: the first ranks steps by gain per unit of budget; the second by gain per unit of
\emph{both} shared resources, $\Delta g / (\Delta x/B + \lambda\, p_i \Delta x / L)$, with
$\lambda$ chosen from a small grid --- a Lagrangian-style pricing of the loss cap.

The budget-only rule reaches a median of \GreedyMed\% of the optimum, and as little as
\GreedyTightest\% when the loss cap is tight (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}): it spends the
scarce loss allowance on high-risk farmers. Pricing the loss cap recovers \GreedyRiskMed\%
(worst case \GreedyRiskMin\%). The practical consequence is that cheap allocation is good
allocation \emph{only with a decent estimate of $p_i$} --- which is what a repayment history
could supply.

\section{A revolving-fund simulation}

\paragraph{Model.} A fund lends to 1{,}000 farmers in 20 villages over four seasons and
relends what is repaid. Farm sizes follow the forest-zone distribution in
\citet{chamberlin2008} (median 1.62\,ha); yields and maize prices are Ghana's 2024 figures
(2.60\,t/ha; GH\textcent{}9{,}286/t; \citealp{srid2024}); 18\% of farmers do not respond to
fertiliser \citep{ichami2019}. A loan is diverted with probability equal to the leakage
rate of its custody model (5\% custodial, 30\% farmer-held keys; assumptions motivated by
Section~2). Inputs raise the harvest by
$r_i s_i\,(1-e^{-x/s_i})$, scaled by a village and an individual weather shock. Default
follows
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{default}) = \sigma\!\left(\alpha + z_i + \delta\,\text{diverted} - \gamma\,\text{shock}
- \kappa\,(\text{gain}/\text{amount due} - 1)\right),
\end{equation}
where $z_i$ is an unobserved repayment type and the last term is repayment capacity.

\paragraph{The return to credit is the most uncertain input.} Agronomic arithmetic
(18\,kg grain per kg N at 2024 prices) suggests a gross return near 8.8 per cedi; on-farm
trials in northern Ghana returned about 3.6 \citep[Fosu and Dittoh 2011, as reported in][]{karlan2014};
and a farmer-level RCT found cash grants raised harvest value by an amount not
distinguishable from zero, concluding that uninsured risk, not credit, is the binding
constraint \citep{karlan2014}. All three are run as scenarios. The default anchor (25\%)
sits inside a range whose upper end, 58\%, is the only Ghanaian pilot figure found
\citep{karlan2010}.

\paragraph{Policies.} \emph{Equal} amounts; risk-aware greedy with one pooled default rate
(\emph{no history}); the same with a logistic model of default on the ledger history
(\emph{ledger}); and the same knowing each farmer's type (\emph{known type}, a benchmark,
not attainable). Every policy runs on identical random draws, \SimReps{} replications per
scenario.

\paragraph{Findings.} Pricing risk beats equal amounts by \TargetLo--\TargetHi\% in every
scenario. Ledger history adds a further \LedgerLo--\LedgerHi\%, except where returns are
so high that defaults are rare (\LedgerMin\%): there is little to learn when almost everyone
repays. Four seasons of history captures only part of what knowing farmers would be
worth (under RCT-like returns, \RctLedgerPct\% of a possible \RctTypePct\%). Custody
dominates allocation: farmer-held keys cost \CustodyLo--\CustodyHi\% of the gain under the
ledger policy. And no rule rescues the fund when returns are RCT-like: it keeps
\FundRctLo--\FundRctHi\% of its capital after four seasons under every policy, including the
benchmark --- consistent with \citet{karlan2014}: when risk binds, better targeting of
credit is not enough.

The repayment record carries the information: with custodial wallets, five seasons of loan
and default history match the known-type benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:auc}). Seeing
diversion adds almost nothing --- even seeing \emph{all} of it, which no ledger can (S7). In
the full simulation, moving the on-ledger share of diversion from none to all changes the
ledger policy's gain only from \ObsZero{} to \ObsFull{}. Diversion is better
\emph{prevented}, by custody, than \emph{detected}. Two cautions: gradient boosting does
worse than logistic regression on this little data (\GbmAuc{} vs \LogAuc{}), and the
logistic model is over-confident at the extremes (lowest decile predicted \CalLoPred{},
observed \CalLoObs{}; highest \CalHiPred{} vs \CalHiObs{}), which matters because allocation
uses $p_i$ directly against the loss cap. Labels exist only for farmers who received a
loan, so a model trained on a selective lender's book inherits its choices; randomising
part of the allocation is the standard remedy.

\section{Robustness}

Each of fifteen unsourced assumptions was moved to a low and a high value
(\SensReps{} replications per point; Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}). No headline result changes
sign except the fund's survival, which ranges from \SensFundLo{} to \SensFundHi{} times its
initial capital and cannot be claimed either way. Ledger history is worth between
+\SensLedgerLo\% and +\SensLedgerHi\%; it vanishes when defaults are rare
(+\SensDefLow\%) or the loss cap is loose (+\SensCapLoose\%) and is largest when defaults
are high (+\SensDefHigh\%) or the cap is tight (+\SensCapTight\%). The history is worth
most exactly where lending is hardest. Self-custody always costs \SensCustLo--\SensCustHi\%
of the gain; risk-aware targeting always beats equal amounts (+\SensTargetLo\% to
+\SensTargetHi\%). The on-ledger share of diversion is the least influential assumption.
One-at-a-time ranges miss interactions; a joint design is the natural next step.

\section{How a field experiment could test this}

The simulation produces hypotheses, not evidence. The two design choices it singles out
--- custody and allocation --- can be randomised. A cluster-randomised trial would assign
\emph{villages}, not farmers, because weather is shared within a village and farmers trade
with one another (spillovers through S4, S6 and S7 are local).

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{Arms.} Custodial versus farmer-held wallets, cross-randomised with
history-based versus equal allocation. A third, cross-cutting arm bundling index
insurance would test the \citet{karlan2014} result directly in a token-credit setting.
\item \textbf{Outcomes.} Vendor redemptions (on-ledger, near-free to measure), yields by
crop cuts, repayment, and fund capital; the ledger supplies the first, the field the rest.
\item \textbf{Estimand.} Average treatment effects by arm, with standard errors clustered
by village; pre-registered, since the simulation already names the expected signs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}

All farmer data are synthetic, and behavioural parameters --- shock sizes, the response of
default to type, diversion and capacity, leakage by custody model --- are assumptions,
bounded but not estimated. Harvest gain is valued output, not profit: family labour and
input costs beyond the loan are not netted out. Landholding data are from 2005/06; the
urea price behind the naive return scenario comes from an unverified source and is used
only as an upper bound. Testnet behaviour was verified for the amendments enabled on
2 October 2026 and may change. Nothing here describes the internals of any deployed
system.

\section{Conclusion}

On the XRP Ledger a spending restriction is only as strong as the custody of the farmer's
key, and a vendor willing to collude defeats any ledger rule. Within the model, what the
ledger offers a lender is not surveillance of spending but a shared, tamper-evident
repayment record --- worth most where lending is hardest, and worth little if the
underlying return to inputs is low. Which of these hold for real farmers is a question for a
field trial, and the simulation says which trial would be informative.
