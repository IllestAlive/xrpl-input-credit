Restricted-spend input credit on the XRP Ledger

What the ledger enforces, and what its history is worth.

Credit given to smallholder farmers as tokens that can only be spent with approved input vendors combines a ledger mechanism with an economic bet. This page explores both: testnet experiments on what the XRP Ledger enforces, a budget-constrained allocation model, and a simulation of a revolving fund that asks how much a lender gains from the repayment history the ledger accumulates.

1. What the ledger enforces

Real testnet transactions with fresh accounts for every run: an issuer with RequireAuth, an approved vendor, two farmers with DepositAuth, and an unauthorised stranger.

Escape routes

Findings

  1. DepositAuth alone is not enough. A farmer can push credit into another farmer's account by rippling a self-payment through it, and the receiving farmer signs nothing (S4a). The farmer's issuer line must also carry NoRipple (S4b).
  2. Every farmer-side protection sits behind the farmer's key (S4c, S5). Whether the restriction holds is a question of custody, not of the ledger.
  3. The open DEX turns credit into cash (S6a). A permissioned domain restricts its own order book, not where the token trades (S6b, S6c).
  4. The issuer's tools work but are blunt (S8): freeze also stops legitimate spending; clawback can take any holder's balance.
  5. Vendor collusion is invisible on-ledger (S7): it looks exactly like S1.

6. The feature-phone channel

Few smallholder farmers have smartphones, which is why mobile trading platforms in the region reach farmers over USSD and SMS on basic phones (Neza, Nyarko and Orozco 2023). A basic phone cannot hold an XRP Ledger key or sign a transaction, so an operator must hold it: the custodial case. That is exactly where the ledger's restrictions hold, because the farmer has no way to clear DepositAuth or NoRipple (S4c, S5). The price is trust in the operator and the issuer, who can still freeze and claw back (S8).
